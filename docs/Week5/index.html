Week 5: Probability and Uncertainty in Analysis

IA 240: Technology Applications in a Networked World

Intelligence analysis is reasoning under uncertainty. This lecture formalizes that reasoning with probability theory — covering classical and Bayesian probability, the rules of probability, and how explicit probabilistic reasoning corrects systematic cognitive errors that distort analytical judgment. The probabilistic language standards used by the Intelligence Community are grounded in the formal framework introduced here.

  • If amateurs using only open sources can out-predict analysts with classified access, what does that suggest actually drives forecasting accuracy — information, or method?

Part I: What is Probability?

Defining Probability

Probability is a mathematical framework for quantifying uncertainty. It gives analysts a rigorous, communicable language for expressing the likelihood of events—from coin flips to geopolitical crises.

The classical definition:

\[P(A) = \frac{\text{Number of Favorable Outcomes}}{\text{Total Number of Possible Outcomes}}\]

where \(P(A)\) is the probability that event \(A\) occurs, constrained to the range \([0, 1]\):

  • \(P(A) = 0\): event is impossible
  • \(P(A) = 1\): event is certain
  • \(P(A) = 0.5\): equally likely to occur or not occur
NoteWhy This Matters for Intelligence Analysis

Intelligence analysis is fundamentally about reasoning under uncertainty. Explicit probabilistic language—“we assess with high confidence (>85%) that…”—is more precise and less manipulable than vague hedges like “likely” or “probably.” The Intelligence Community uses standardized probability ranges for exactly this reason.

Sample Space

The sample space (\(S\) or \(\Omega\)) is the set of all possible outcomes for a given experiment or scenario.

Properties:

  • Must be exhaustive—every possible outcome is represented
  • Outcomes must be mutually exclusive—no two can occur simultaneously
Experiment Sample Space
Roll a six-sided die \(\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\)
Flip a coin \(\{H, T\}\)
A country holds an election: incumbent wins or loses \(\{\text{Win}, \text{Loss}\}\)
A state either acquires nuclear weapons or does not within 5 years \(\{\text{Acquire}, \neg\text{Acquire}\}\)

Outcomes: Simple and Compound

Simple Outcome
A single point in the sample space. Example: rolling a “3” on a die or one candidate winning an election between two opponents.
Compound Outcome
A subset of the sample space containing more than one point. Example: rolling an odd number (1, 3, or 5). In intelligence terms: “a conflict occurs” might encompass multiple distinct pathways (border skirmish, cyber attack, proxy conflict).

Part II: Rules of Probability

NoteNotation: Set Symbols Used in This Section
Symbol Read as Meaning
\(A \cup B\) “A union B” (A or B) Either A or B occurs — or both
\(A \cap B\) “A intersect B” (A and B) Both A and B occur together
\(P(B \mid A)\) “P of B given A” Probability of B, given that A has already occurred (the vertical bar \(\mid\) means “given”)

The Addition Rule

Used to find the probability that at least one of two events occurs (A or B, or both):

\[P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\]

So, the probability that A has occurred or B has occured, minus the double counting where both events occur simultaneously.

Example: Drawing a card from a standard 52-card deck — probability it is a King or a Heart:

\[P(\text{King} \cup \text{Heart}) = \frac{4}{52} + \frac{13}{52} - \frac{1}{52} = \frac{16}{52} = \frac{4}{13} \approx 0.308\]

There are 4 Kings, 13 Hearts, and 1 King of Hearts (which is counted in both categories and we need to take out) in a standard deck, or 16 favorable outcomes out of 52 possible outcomes.

TipSpecial Case: Mutually Exclusive Events

If \(A\) and \(B\) cannot both occur (\(P(A \cap B) = 0\)), the addition rule simplifies to \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)\).

Example: Drawing one card from a standard deck — probability it is a King or a Queen. A single card cannot be both, so the events are mutually exclusive and no subtraction is needed:

\[P(\text{King} \cup \text{Queen}) = \frac{4}{52} + \frac{4}{52} = \frac{8}{52} = \frac{2}{13} \approx 0.154\]

The Multiplication Rule

Used to find the probability that both event A and event B occur:

\[P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B \mid A)\]

where \(P(B \mid A)\) is the conditional probability of B given that A has occurred.

Example: Rolling a 6 then flipping heads:

\[P(6 \text{ and } H) = P(6) \cdot P(H \mid 6) = \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{12} \approx 0.083\]

In this example, the two events are independent, so \(P(6) \approx 0.16\) for the dice and \(P(H) = 0.5\) for the coin flip. But, both events need to occur. So, intitatively, the probablily of both events should be lower than the probability of either event alone.

WarningParlay Bets — The Multiplication Rule in Action

Sports parlays require multiple independent bets to hit in order to win. The probability compounds multiplicatively. For example, a 3-leg parlay with each leg having a 50% chance of winning has only a 12.5% chance of hitting the entire parlay. This is why parlays generated roughly 72.5% of sportsbook gross revenue!1

This is not coincidence. Every additional leg of a parlay multiplies the probability of losing.

Part III: Bayesian vs. Frequentist Thinking

The Coin Flip Thought Experiment

Consider a fair coin. Before the flip, \(P(\text{Heads}) = 0.5\). Now imagine I flip the coin, cover it with my hand, and ask: “What is the probability it is heads?”

  • For a frequentist, the coin is already determined: the question has an unknown but fixed answer; probability, in the formal sense, does not apply to this realized event.
  • For a Bayesian, your probability (0.5) represents your epistemic state: your uncertainty about the outcome. If someone else peeked under my hand, their probability would be 0 or 1. Probability describes knowledge, not just the world.

The key insight: probability describes our knowledge about the world, not solely objective features of the world itself.

Comparing the Two Frameworks

Frequentist

  • Probability = long-run relative frequency of outcomes over repeated trials (e.g., the Pittsburgh Pirates will win 50% of the time if they play 1000 games)
  • The parameter being estimated is fixed, not a random variable (e.g., the true proportion of heads)
  • Cannot assign probability to hypotheses directly
  • Computes: “If the null hypothesis were true, how likely is data this extreme?” → the p-value
  • Never directly tells you whether the hypothesis is true, just the likelihood of observing the data under that hypothesis (e.g., “If the null hypothesis were true, the probability of observing this data is 0.03”)

Bayesian

  • Probability = degree of rational belief, updated as evidence accumulates (e.g., the Pirates are 70% likely to win the World Series this year, given their current roster and performance)
  • Parameters are random variables that can have probability distributions (e.g., the probability distribution of the proportion of heads)
  • Can compute \(P(\text{Hypothesis} \mid \text{Evidence})\) directly
  • Requires specifying a prior - a belief before evidence (e.g., “I believe the Pirates have a 10% chance of winning the World Series before the season starts”)
  • Updates priors to posteriors using observed data (e.g., “Given the Pirates’ current performance, I now believe they have a 70% chance of winning the World Series”)
NoteWhich Approach Is “Right”?

Both frameworks have strong theoretical and practical justifications. Frequentist methods dominate classical statistics and much of published social science. Bayesian methods are increasingly prominent in areas where incorporating prior knowledge explicitly is desirable and updating beliefs as evidence accumulates is the primary goal (does this sound like it would be useful in intelligence analysis?).

You’ve misplaced your phone somewhere in your home. Pressing a button causes the phone to beep but the sound is faint and muffled. How would you go about finding it? Does your approach reflect frequentist or Bayesian reasoning?

Part IV: The Frequentist Framework

The Core Logic

Unlike Bayesian statistics, the frequentist school does not begin by assigning probabilities to hypotheses. Instead, it treats the underlying population parameter as fixed but unknown and evaluates how unusual the observed data would be under a specified hypothesis. For example, an analyst might want to determine whether a recent increase in military exercises is statistically unusual compared with the state’s historical behavior. Using a frequentist approach, the analyst would ask how likely an increase this large would be if there were no real change in the state’s underlying military posture.

The process usually begins with two competing hypotheses:

\[H_0: \text{The null hypothesis}\]

\[H_A: \text{The alternative hypothesis}\]

The null hypothesis typically represents no effect, no difference, or no relationship - the status quo. The alternative hypothesis represents the possibility that an effect, difference, or relationship exists.

For example, suppose we want to determine whether students who use an AI study tool score higher on an exam:

\[H_0: \mu_{\text{AI}} - \mu_{\text{No AI}} = 0\]

\[H_A: \mu_{\text{AI}} - \mu_{\text{No AI}} > 0\]

Where…

\(H_0\)
is the null hypothesis. It represents the default claim that there is no difference between the groups.
\(H_A\)
is the alternative hypothesis. It represents that relationship between the groups is present.
\(\mu\)
the Greek letter mu, representing a population mean, or the true average for the entire population.
\(\mu_{\text{AI}}\)
the true average outcome for people who use AI.
\(\mu_{\text{No AI}}\)
the true average outcome for people who do not use AI.

For \(H_0\), we would expect no difference in scores between the group using AI and the group not using AI (hence the = sign). For \(H_A\), we would expect that one group (either the AI user or non-users) to have a difference score (hence the > sign).

The frequentist question is not:

What is the probability that the null hypothesis is true?

Instead, it asks:

If the null hypothesis were true, how unusual would it be to observe data like these?

This logic is commonly expressed using a test statistic and a p-value.

\[p\text{-value} = P(\text{result as extreme as observed} \mid H_0)\]

More formally:

\[p = P(T \geq T_{\text{observed}} \mid H_0)\]

where \(T\) represents the test statistic. For a two-sided test, results that are equally extreme in either direction are included.

Key Terms for Statistical Testing

\(H_0\) — Null Hypothesis
The default claim being evaluated. It usually states that there is no effect, no difference, or no relationship in the population.
\(H_A\) — Alternative Hypothesis
The competing claim. It states that an effect, difference, or relationship exists. It may be directional, such as “greater than,” or nondirectional, such as “not equal to.”
Population Parameter
A fixed but unknown characteristic of the full population, such as a population mean, proportion, correlation, or regression coefficient. Frequentist methods use sample data to estimate or test claims about this parameter.
Sample Statistic
A value calculated from the observed sample, such as a sample mean, sample proportion, or difference between two sample means. Sample statistics vary from sample to sample.
Test Statistic
A standardized measure of how far the observed result is from what would be expected under the null hypothesis. Common examples include the \(z\), \(t\), \(\chi^2\), and \(F\) statistics.
Sampling Distribution
The distribution of a statistic across hypothetical repeated samples drawn under the same conditions. It shows how much a statistic would vary because of random sampling.
\(p\)-value
The probability of observing a result at least as extreme as the one obtained, assuming the null hypothesis is true. A small p-value indicates that the observed data are difficult to reconcile with the null hypothesis.
\(\alpha\) — Significance Level
The threshold selected before the analysis for deciding whether a result is statistically significant. A common choice is \(\alpha = 0.05\).
Confidence Interval
A range of plausible values for the population parameter constructed using a method that captures the true parameter at a specified rate over repeated samples. A 95% confidence procedure will contain the true parameter in approximately 95% of repeated samples.
Standard Error
An estimate of how much a sample statistic would vary across repeated samples. Smaller standard errors indicate more precise estimates.

The Frequentist Decision Rule

Before conducting the test, the researcher selects a significance level:

\[\alpha = 0.05\]

The observed p-value is then compared with \(\alpha\):

\[ \begin{cases} p \leq \alpha & \text{Reject } H_0 \ p > \alpha & \text{Fail to reject } H_0 \end{cases} \]

If the p-value is less than or equal to the significance level, the result is described as statistically significant.

If the p-value is greater than the significance level, the researcher fails to reject the null hypothesis.

Failing to reject the null hypothesis does not prove that the null hypothesis is true. It means that the observed evidence is not sufficiently inconsistent with the null hypothesis under the selected decision rule.

WarningDo Not “Accept” the Null Hypothesis

Frequentist testing usually leads to one of two conclusions:

  • Reject the null hypothesis
  • Fail to reject the null hypothesis

A nonsignificant result does not demonstrate that there is no effect. The study may have had too little data, too much noise, or too little statistical power to detect the effect.

The Role of Repeated Sampling

The frequentist framework defines probability in terms of long-run frequencies. Imagine repeating the same study many times using new random samples from the same population.

Each sample would produce a slightly different:

  • Sample mean
  • Test statistic
  • p-value
  • Confidence interval

Frequentist procedures are evaluated according to how well they perform across these hypothetical repetitions.

For example, if the null hypothesis is true and researchers repeatedly use a significance level of \(\alpha = 0.05\), they will incorrectly reject the null hypothesis approximately 5% of the time in the long run.

This is known as a Type I error.

Errors in Hypothesis Testing

Because decisions are made using incomplete sample data, errors are possible.

Reality Fail to Reject \(H_0\) Reject \(H_0\)
\(H_0\) is true Correct decision Type I error
\(H_0\) is false Type II error Correct decision
Type I Error
Rejecting the null hypothesis when it is actually true. This is sometimes called a false positive. The probability of a Type I error is controlled by \(\alpha\).
Type II Error
Failing to reject the null hypothesis when it is actually false. This is sometimes called a false negative. Its probability is represented by \(\beta\).

Applied Example: Does an AI Study Tool Improve Scores?

Suppose students who use an AI study tool score an average of 4 points higher than students who do not use it.

The hypotheses are:

\[H_0: \mu_{\text{AI}} - \mu_{\text{No AI}} = 0\]

\[H_A: \mu_{\text{AI}} - \mu_{\text{No AI}} > 0\]

After conducting a statistical test, the researcher obtains:

\[p = 0.03\]

Using a significance level of:

\[\alpha = 0.05\]

we compare the two values:

\[0.03 < 0.05\]

The researcher therefore rejects the null hypothesis and concludes that the data provide statistically significant evidence that students using the AI study tool score higher.

The correct interpretation is:

If the AI study tool truly produced no improvement, there would be a 3% probability of observing a difference this large or larger because of random sampling variation.

The following interpretations would be incorrect:

  • “There is a 3% probability that the null hypothesis is true.”
  • “There is a 97% probability that the AI study tool works.”
  • “The AI study tool has been proven effective.”
  • “The improvement is necessarily large or educationally meaningful.”

The p-value measures how surprising the evidence is under the null hypothesis. It does not directly measure the probability that either hypothesis is true.

TipStatistical Significance Is Not Practical Significance

A result can be statistically significant but too small to matter in practice. Large samples can make very small effects statistically detectable.

A complete analysis should therefore report:

  • The estimated effect
  • The confidence interval
  • The p-value
  • The effect size
  • The practical importance of the result

Part V: Bayes’ Theorem

The Formula

Bayes’ Theorem formalizes how to update probabilities given new evidence. We have two events, A and B, and we want to know the probability of A given that B has occurred:

\[P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A)}{P(B)}\]

Or, if you prefer, we can think about it as Hypothesis (H) and Evidence (E):

\[P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H) \cdot P(H)}{P(E)}\]

\(P(H)\) — Prior
Your probability for the hypothesis before observing the evidence. Represents existing knowledge, base rates, or initial belief.
\(P(H \mid E)\) — Posterior
Your updated probability for the hypothesis after observing the evidence. This is what you want.
\(P(E \mid H)\) — Likelihood
The probability of observing this evidence if the hypothesis were true. How consistent is this evidence with the hypothesis?
\(P(E)\) — Marginal Probability of Evidence
The total probability of observing this evidence across all hypotheses. Acts as a normalizing constant.

When to Use Bayes’ Theorem

Apply Bayesian reasoning when you have:

  1. A hypothesis (an uncertain claim about the world)
  2. Evidence (new observations)
  3. A desire to estimate \(P(\text{Hypothesis} \mid \text{Evidence})\)

Applied Example: Does an AI Study App Improve Quiz Scores?

Suppose a professor wants to determine whether a new AI-powered study app helps students perform better on weekly quizzes. Half of the students use the app, while the other half use their usual study methods.

Frequentist Approach

A frequentist treats the app’s true effect as a fixed but unknown value. The professor begins with a null hypothesis:

The study app does not improve quiz scores.

After comparing the two groups, the professor calculates a p-value. The p-value asks:

If the app truly had no effect, how unusual would it be to observe a difference this large—or larger—just by random chance?

If the p-value is sufficiently small, the professor rejects the null hypothesis. The professor might conclude that the results provide evidence that the app improves performance.

The frequentist approach does not assign a probability to the hypothesis itself. It would therefore be incorrect to say, “There is a 95% probability that the app works.” Instead, the analysis evaluates how compatible the observed data are with the assumption that the app has no effect.

Bayesian Approach

A Bayesian begins with a prior belief about the app’s likely effectiveness. This prior might be based on previous studies of similar tools, earlier semesters, an expectation that most educational apps produce only modest improvements, the company’s marketing claims, or just a gut feeling.

The professor then combines that prior belief with the new quiz-score data.

  • Prior: beliefs about the app’s effectiveness before examining the current class
  • Evidence: the difference in quiz scores between students who used the app and those who did not
  • Posterior: updated beliefs about the app’s effectiveness after considering the new results

The Bayesian analysis might produce a conclusion such as:

There is an 87% probability that the app improves average quiz scores, but only a 35% probability that the improvement is greater than five percentage points.

This allows the professor to distinguish between an effect that probably exists and an effect that is large enough to matter.

The Key Difference

The two approaches ask related but different questions:

  • Frequentist: How unusual would these results be if the app had no effect?
  • Bayesian: Given what we believed before and the results we observed, how probable are the different possible effects of the app?

Frequentist reasoning evaluates the behavior of evidence across hypothetical repeated studies. Bayesian reasoning updates uncertainty about the app’s effectiveness as new evidence becomes available.

NoteBayesian Approach - Let’s Walk Through the Math

Let \(\delta\) represent the app’s effect on average quiz scores:

\[ \delta = \mu_{\text{App}} - \mu_{\text{No App}} \]

A positive value means that students using the app score higher. For example:

  • \(\delta = 0\) means the app has no effect
  • \(\delta = 2\) means the app raises scores by 2 percentage points
  • \(\delta = 5\) means the app raises scores by 5 percentage points

Bayes’ Theorem can be written as:

\[ P(\delta \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid \delta) \cdot P(\delta)}{P(E)}\]

where:

  • \(P(\delta)\) is the prior distribution for the app’s effect
  • \(P(E \mid \delta)\) is the likelihood of observing the quiz data for each possible effect
  • \(P(\delta \mid E)\) is the posterior distribution
  • \(P(E)\) is the marginal probability of the evidence, which ensures that the posterior probabilities sum to one

In proportional form, which is often more useful for understanding the calculation:

\[ P(\delta \mid E) \propto P(E \mid \delta) \cdot P(\delta) \]

Or more simply:

\[ \text{Posterior} \propto \text{Likelihood} \times \text{Prior} \]

Step 1: Establish the Prior

Suppose previous research suggests that educational apps usually produce small, positive effects. Before collecting data from the current class, the professor represents the app’s effect with a normal prior distribution:

\[ \delta \sim N(2, 3^2) \]

This prior has:

\[ \mu_{\text{prior}} = 2 \]

\[ \sigma_{\text{prior}} = 3 \]

The prior expresses a belief that the most likely effect is an improvement of approximately 2 percentage points, although effects moderately below or above that value remain plausible.

Because the prior distribution includes values below zero, the professor is not assuming that the app must work. The prior allows for the possibility that the app has no effect or even harms performance.

Step 2: Collect the First Quiz Data

After several quizzes, suppose the professor observes that students using the app score an average of 4 percentage points higher than students who do not use it.

The estimated effect is:

\[ \hat{\delta} = 4 \]

Suppose the standard error of this estimate is:

\[ SE = 2 \]

The likelihood can therefore be represented as:

\[ E \mid \delta \sim N(\delta, 2^2) \]

The likelihood is centered on the observed 4-point difference. However, because the estimate has uncertainty, nearby effects such as 2, 3, 5, or 6 points are also reasonably consistent with the data.

Step 3: Update the Prior

When both the prior and likelihood are normally distributed, the posterior mean can be calculated using a precision-weighted average:

\[ \mu_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{ \frac{\mu_{\text{prior}}}{\sigma_{\text{prior}}^2} + \frac{\hat{\delta}}{SE^2} }{ \frac{1}{\sigma_{\text{prior}}^2} + \frac{1}{SE^2} } \]

Substituting the values:

\[ \mu_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{ \frac{2}{3^2} + \frac{4}{2^2} }{ \frac{1}{3^2} + \frac{1}{2^2} } \]

\[ \mu_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{ \frac{2}{9} + \frac{4}{4} }{ \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{4} } \approx 3.38 \]

The posterior variance is:

\[ \sigma_{\text{posterior}}^2 = \frac{1}{ \frac{1}{\sigma_{\text{prior}}^2} + \frac{1}{SE^2} } \]

Substituting the values:

\[ \sigma_{\text{posterior}}^2 = \frac{1}{ \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{4} } \approx 2.77 \]

Therefore:

\[ \sigma_{\text{posterior}} \approx 1.66 \]

The updated posterior distribution is approximately:

\[ \delta \mid E \sim N(3.38, 1.66^2) \]

The professor’s best estimate has therefore moved from a 2-point improvement before seeing the data to approximately a 3.38-point improvement after seeing the data.

At the same time, uncertainty has decreased:

\[ \sigma_{\text{prior}} = 3 \]

\[ \sigma_{\text{posterior}} = 1.66 \]

The data have made the professor more confident about the range of plausible effects.

Interpreting the First Posterior

The posterior distribution can answer questions that are directly relevant to the professor.

What is the probability that the app helps at all?

The professor calculates:

\[ P(\delta > 0 \mid E) \]

Based on the posterior distribution, this probability is approximately:

\[ P(\delta > 0 \mid E) \approx 0.98 \]

The professor could therefore conclude:

Given the prior assumptions and the quiz data, there is approximately a 98% probability that the app improves average quiz scores.

What is the probability that the effect is educationally meaningful?

Suppose the professor decides that an improvement must exceed 5 percentage points to be large enough to justify adopting the app across the course.

The relevant probability is:

\[ P(\delta > 5 \mid E) \]

Based on the posterior distribution:

\[ P(\delta > 5 \mid E) \approx 0.17 \]

The professor could therefore conclude:

There is strong evidence that the app provides some benefit, but only about a 17% probability that the improvement exceeds 5 percentage points.

This distinction is important. An effect may probably exist without being large enough to matter in practice.

Step 4: Update Again After More Quizzes

Bayesian analysis is sequential. The posterior from one round of analysis can become the prior for the next round.

Suppose the professor collects a second, independent batch of quiz results later in the semester. In this batch, students using the app score an average of 6 percentage points higher:

\[ \hat{\delta}_{\text{new}} = 6 \]

Because more quizzes and students are now included, the estimate is more precise:

\[ SE_{\text{new}} = 1.2 \]

The previous posterior becomes the new prior:

\[ \delta \sim N(3.38, 1.66^2) \]

The professor updates again:

\[ \mu_{\text{new posterior}} = \frac{ \frac{3.38}{1.66^2} + \frac{6}{1.2^2} }{ \frac{1}{1.66^2} + \frac{1}{1.2^2} } \]

This produces a new posterior mean of approximately:

\[ \mu_{\text{new posterior}} \approx 5.11 \]

The new posterior standard deviation is approximately:

\[ \sigma_{\text{new posterior}} \approx 0.97 \]

The updated distribution is therefore:

\[ \delta \mid E_{\text{all}} \sim N(5.11, 0.97^2) \]

The additional quizzes have produced two changes:

  1. The estimated effect has moved upward, from approximately 3.38 to 5.11 percentage points.
  2. The uncertainty has narrowed, from a posterior standard deviation of approximately 1.66 to 0.97.

The probability that the app provides any benefit is now greater than 99%:

\[ P(\delta > 0 \mid E_{\text{all}}) > 0.99 \]

The probability that the improvement exceeds the professor’s 5-point threshold is now approximately:

\[ P(\delta > 5 \mid E_{\text{all}}) \approx 0.54 \]

The professor could now conclude:

After incorporating the additional quiz data, there is greater than a 99% probability that the app improves scores and approximately a 54% probability that the improvement exceeds five percentage points.

The new evidence did not cause the professor to discard the previous analysis. Instead, it updated the existing posterior distribution, shifting the most plausible effect upward and reducing uncertainty.

TipProbability of an Effect vs. Probability of a Meaningful Effect

The posterior allows the professor to ask more than whether the effect is above zero.

The professor can calculate:

\[ P(\delta > 0 \mid E) \]

to determine the probability that the app helps at all, and:

\[ P(\delta > 5 \mid E) \]

to determine the probability that the app produces a substantively meaningful improvement.

This separates three different conclusions:

  • The app probably has no effect.
  • The app probably has a small effect.
  • The app probably has a large enough effect to justify adoption.
ImportantBayesian Updating Is Sequential

Bayesian reasoning allows evidence to accumulate over time:

\[ \text{Initial Prior} \rightarrow \text{First Evidence} \rightarrow \text{First Posterior} \]

The first posterior can then become the prior for the next update:

\[ \text{First Posterior} \rightarrow \text{New Evidence} \rightarrow \text{New Posterior} \]

This makes Bayesian analysis especially useful when data arrive gradually, such as weekly quizzes, polling results, intelligence reports, medical tests, or sensor observations.

Example: Librarian or Farmer?

Steve is shy, withdrawn, tidy, and has a passion for detail. Is he more likely a librarian or a farmer?2

Most people intuitively say “librarian”—but the base rate matters. In the United States, there are roughly 200 farmers for every librarian. Even if the description fits librarians far better than farmers on average, the overwhelming numerical advantage of farmers dominates.

Using Bayes:

  • Priors
    • \(P(\text{Librarian}) = \frac{1}{201} \approx 0.005\);
    • \(P(\text{Farmer}) = \frac{200}{201} \approx 0.995\)
  • After applying a likelihood ratio that the description fits librarians ~40× better than farmers:
    • \(P(\text{Librarian} \mid \text{Description}) \approx 17\%\)
    • \(P(\text{Farmer} \mid \text{Description}) \approx 83\%\)

The lesson: base rates are evidence. Ignoring them (base rate neglect) is a well-documented cognitive bias that Bayesian reasoning directly corrects.

Worked Example 2: Fire and Smoke

Given:

  • Dangerous fires are rare: \(P(\text{Fire}) = 0.01\)
  • Smoke is fairly common: \(P(\text{Smoke}) = 0.10\) (due to barbecues, etc.)
  • Fires almost always produce smoke: \(P(\text{Smoke} \mid \text{Fire}) = 0.90\)

Question: \(P(\text{Fire} \mid \text{Smoke}) = \;?\)

Solution: \[P(\text{Fire} \mid \text{Smoke}) = \frac{P(\text{Smoke} \mid \text{Fire}) \cdot P(\text{Fire})}{P(\text{Smoke})}\] \[= \frac{0.90 \times 0.01}{0.10} = \frac{0.009}{0.10} = 0.09\]

Even with smoke present, there is only a 9% probability of a dangerous fire. This reflects the power of low base rates.

The Core Principle

Evidence should not determine beliefs—it should update them.

Look at all cases where the evidence is true. What proportion of those cases also feature the hypothesis? That proportion is your posterior.

Part V: Application to Intelligence Analysis

The Intelligence Context

Intelligence analysis is fundamentally an exercise in reasoning under uncertainty. Analysts rarely observe an adversary’s intentions directly; instead, they infer them from evidence that is fragmentary, ambiguous, and sometimes deliberately deceptive. Nearly every judgment an analyst makes — whether a state intends to escalate, whether a program is weapons-related, whether a group has the capability to act — is at bottom a probability estimate about an unobserved reality rather than a statement of certainty. For this reason, the Intelligence Community formalizes uncertainty in its analytic standards. Intelligence Community Directive 203 requires analysts to express and calibrate the likelihood of their judgments and to keep that likelihood distinct from the confidence they hold in the underlying sourcing.3

Bayes’ Theorem gives this practice a formal structure. The prior encodes what analysts already believed — from history, doctrine, and earlier collection — before the newest reporting arrived. The likelihood captures how consistent that new reporting is with each competing hypothesis. The posterior is the revised judgment. Framed this way, the analyst’s task is not to ask whether a single report proves a conclusion, but how far it should move an existing belief. The same situation can also be approached through a frequentist lens, and the two frameworks ask subtly different questions of the same evidence.

NoteA Recent Example

Scenario: Concern has risen following Russian drone incursions into Polish airspace.

Frequentist framing: If Russia had no intent to escalate against Poland, what is the probability we would observe drone incursions at this frequency? (Tests consistency of evidence with a null hypothesis.)

Bayesian framing: Given our prior belief about the probability of Russian hostile action against Poland, how should our belief change after observing the drone incursions? (Updates beliefs in light of evidence.)

Both framings are useful. The Bayesian framing more naturally incorporates what we already knew; the frequentist framing is useful for hypothesis rejection under formal uncertainty.

WarningCommon Analytical Failures Addressed by Bayesian Thinking
  • Ignoring base rates — overweighting vivid evidence and underweighting background frequencies
  • Confirmation bias — only updating in response to confirming evidence; discounting disconfirming evidence
  • Anchoring — being insufficiently responsive to new evidence because the prior is too strong
  • Representativeness heuristic — judging likelihood by how “typical” something looks, rather than by actual probabilities (the Steve/librarian problem)

Suggested Readings

Jervis, R. (2006). Reports, politics, and intelligence failures: The case of Iraq. Journal of Strategic Studies, 29(1), 3–52. Access via JMU Library

Post-mortem analysis of the 2002 Iraq WMD assessment. The cognitive biases the Bayesian framework is designed to correct — base rate neglect, confirmation bias, anchoring — appear throughout this case.

Bar-Joseph, U., & Kruglanski, A. W. (2003). Intelligence failure and need for cognitive closure. Political Psychology, 24(1), 75–99. Access via JMU Library

Examines how analysts’ psychological need for certainty leads to premature closure on hypotheses and resistance to disconfirming evidence — exactly what Bayesian reasoning disciplines. Applied to the Yom Kippur War intelligence failure.

Tetlock, P. E. (1999). Theory-driven reasoning about plausible pasts and probable futures in world politics. American Journal of Political Science, 43(2), 335–366. Access via JMU Library

Provides empirical evidence that analysts with more probabilistic, self-correcting reasoning styles outperform those with committed theoretical frameworks on forecasting tasks.

Tetlock, P. E., & Gardner, D. (2015). Superforecasting: How to upgrade your company’s judgment. Harvard Business Review, 93(5), 72–78. Access via JMU Library

Draws on the Good Judgment Project’s findings about calibrated probabilistic forecasting. Relevant to confidence language and the critique of vague uncertainty expressions.
