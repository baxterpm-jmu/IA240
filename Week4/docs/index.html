Week 4: Research Design and Finding Data

IA 240: Technology Applications in a Networked World

Good analysis begins with good questions and a clear understanding of the phenomenon being studied. This lecture covers research design for intelligence problems — hypothesis formulation, methodology selection, variable operationalization — and then introduces ways to find and evaluate data to test those hypotheses.

ImportantRequired Reading Before Tuesday Class

Jonathan Haslam, “How to Explain the KGB’s Amazing Success Identifying CIA Agents in the Field?” Salon (September 26, 2015).

As you read, consider:

  • Totrov turned a vague question (“who is CIA?”) into a set of measurable, observable indicators. How is that process the same as operationalizing variables in a research design — and where could it go wrong (false positives)?
  • The CIA reached for the dramatic hypothesis (a mole) over the simpler structural one (predictable bureaucratic patterns). What does this teach you about generating and testing competing hypotheses before settling on an explanation?
✓

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to:

  • Formulate clear, testable hypotheses by identifying expected relationships among variables, plausible causal mechanisms, and competing explanations.
  • Translate abstract concepts into measurable variables and indicators while identifying the limitations, assumptions, and potential validity problems associated with each operationalization.
  • Design a systematic data-search strategy by breaking a research question into searchable components, developing a broad search vocabulary, and using search operators, specialized databases, repositories, and citation chaining.
  • Evaluate whether a dataset is fit for a specific research purpose by examining its relevance, provenance, coverage, completeness, validity, reliability, timeliness, documentation, and unit of analysis.
  • Document and manage the data-discovery process responsibly by maintaining search and source logs, reading codebooks and licensing terms, preserving original data, and identifying ethical, privacy, and reproducibility concerns.

Part I: The Role of the Hypothesis

Defining Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a testable proposition about a relationship or difference between variables. It represents an analyst’s informed expectation about how the world works — a claim that can be confirmed, disconfirmed, or refined through evidence.

A good hypothesis:

  • Is stated in precise, unambiguous terminology
  • States an expected relationship or difference between two or more variables
  • Is testable — there must exist data that could, in principle, prove it wrong
  • Is grounded in prior knowledge, theory, or literature
NoteHypotheses in Intelligence vs. Academic Research

Academic hypotheses typically take the form: “Higher X is associated with greater Y.” Intelligence hypotheses often take a probabilistic form: “We assess that there is a greater than 60% probability that State A will conduct a ballistic missile test within the next 90 days.” Both require the same underlying logic: a specific claim, derived from prior knowledge, that is falsifiable by evidence.

What Makes a Hypothesis Testable?

For a hypothesis to be testable:

  1. All key concepts must be operationalized — defined in terms of measurable variables
  2. There must exist data capable of producing evidence for or against it
  3. A plausible mechanism must connect the independent variable(s) to the dependent variable
  4. Alternative explanations must be articulable and distinguishable from the primary hypothesis

Part III: Structured Analytic Techniques

Systems thinking (covered in Parts VII–X) produces causal maps. But maps are hypotheses about the world, not descriptions of it. Structured analytic techniques (SATs) complement systems thinking by disciplining how analysts develop and challenge those hypotheses.

Key Assumptions Check (KAC)
A systematic review of the assumptions embedded in a causal loop diagram or analytical assessment. For each causal arrow, there is at least one implicit assumption. A KAC asks: what must be true for this arrow to hold? How confident are we? What would happen if the assumption proved false?
Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH)
A structured technique for evaluating which of several competing hypotheses best fits the available evidence. ACH specifically seeks evidence that is inconsistent with each hypothesis, resisting the natural tendency to seek confirming evidence.
Red Team Analysis
Deliberately adopts an adversary’s perspective to identify vulnerabilities in your own causal model. A red team examining a causal loop diagram asks: which loops could the adversary deliberately influence? Which assumptions in your model does the adversary know about and could exploit?
Indicator Development
Translating a causal loop diagram into a monitoring framework. Each feedback loop predicts observable indicators. Defining those indicators in advance allows analysts to detect whether a loop is activating, accelerating, or being disrupted.

Part IV: Research Methodologies

Quantitative Research

Quantitative research is centered on numerical data and statistical analysis. It seeks to identify patterns, relationships, and trends that can be generalized across populations.

Characteristics: structured, objective, replicable, uses statistical inference.

Common methods: surveys and questionnaires, experiments, secondary data analysis.

NoteSecondary Data Analysis in Intelligence

Much intelligence analysis is secondary data analysis by definition — analysts rarely collect their own data from scratch. Understanding how existing data was collected, cleaned, and coded is therefore essential before drawing conclusions from it.

Qualitative Research

Qualitative research seeks to understand meanings, experiences, and social processes through non-numerical data. It is often exploratory and context-sensitive.

Characteristics: flexible, inductive, produces depth over breadth.

Common methods: interviews, focus groups, case studies — in-depth examination of a single unit to understand broader dynamics.

Mixed-Methods Research

Sequential Designs

One method informs the next. For example: qualitative interviews identify themes → quantitative survey tests those themes at scale.

Concurrent Designs

Both methods run simultaneously and findings are integrated during analysis, producing a holistic picture that neither alone could achieve.

Part VI: Research Design Fundamentals

Operationalization

Operationalization is the process of defining how an abstract concept will be measured in concrete, observable terms.

Abstract Concept Possible Operationalization Limitation
State instability Anti-government demonstrations per year Misses rural unrest; biased toward media-covered events
Military capability Defense spending as % of GDP Ignores efficiency, technology quality, training
Popular support Survey approval ratings Survey conditions may not reflect genuine opinion
Radicalization Attendance at extremist events Underestimates online radicalization
NoteThe Operationalization Problem in Intelligence

Many of the concepts most important to intelligence — resolve, intent, morale, legitimacy — resist clean operationalization. Analysts often use proxy indicators: observable behaviors that correlate with the unmeasurable concept. The key discipline is being explicit about what your proxy does and does not capture.

Unit of Analysis

The unit of analysis is the entity about which you are making claims. Common units in intelligence analysis:

  • Individual — persons of interest, leaders, operatives
  • Organization — armed groups, companies, government agencies
  • Event — attacks, protests, elections, financial transactions
  • State/country — the most common unit in quantitative IR
  • Dyads — pairs of states, organizations, or actors
WarningThe Ecological Fallacy

Drawing conclusions about individuals from aggregate data is the ecological fallacy. If a country has high per capita income and high support for a political movement, it does not follow that wealthy individuals drive that support. Always match the level of your data to the level of your claim.

Levels of Analysis

Level Focus Example Question
Individual Leaders, key decision-makers Does Putin’s risk tolerance differ from his advisors’?
Organizational Bureaucratic/institutional factors How does IRGC organizational culture affect operational decisions?
State Domestic political, economic, military factors Does regime type predict likelihood of WMD pursuit?
Systemic Structure of the international system Does unipolarity reduce great-power conflict?

Variable Types

Dependent Variable (DV)
The outcome you are trying to explain or predict.
Independent Variable (IV)
The factor you believe causes or predicts variation in the DV.
Control Variable
A factor held constant to isolate the IV–DV relationship.
Confounding Variable
A third variable that affects both IV and DV, creating a spurious appearance of causation.
Mediating Variable
A variable through which the IV affects the DV.
TipDiscussion: Operationalizing Threat

In small groups, choose one of the following questions. Identify: (a) the DV and how to measure it, (b) the primary IV and how to operationalize it, (c) at least one likely confounder, and (d) the most significant limitation of your operationalization.

  1. Do states facing existential threats pursue nuclear weapons more frequently?
  2. Does social media use increase the likelihood of mass protests?
  3. Does foreign economic aid reduce armed conflict in recipient countries?

Part V: Getting Started with Finding Data

Finding data is rarely as simple as entering a topic into Google and downloading the first spreadsheet that appears. Researchers must translate a broad information need into a systematic search strategy, identify the types of data that could answer the research question, locate trustworthy sources, evaluate whether the available data are fit for purpose, and document the entire process.

A good data search is therefore part of the research design. The sources you locate will shape:

  • which questions you can answer;
  • which cases, actors, places, or periods you can study;
  • how concepts can be measured;
  • which analytical methods are appropriate;
  • how confident you can be in the findings; and
  • whether someone else can reproduce the analysis.
ImportantData Availability Should Not Determine the Research Question

Researchers sometimes begin with an interesting dataset and then invent a question that can be answered with it. This can be useful for exploratory analysis, but it is not the same as designing a study around a substantive problem.

For example, a researcher might have access to a dataset of social media posts and then ask: “What is the relationship between social media use and political polarization?” But if the research question is: “What factors drive political polarization?” then social media data would likely be insufficient to answer it.

A stronger sequence is:

  1. Define the problem.
  2. Develop the research or intelligence question.
  3. Identify the concepts that must be measured.
  4. Determine what evidence would answer the question.
  5. Search for data that approximate that evidence.
  6. Revise the design when necessary, while clearly documenting the compromises made.
NoteMore on “Identify the concepts that must be measured”

This is where creativity and domain knowledge are essential. You will not always have access to the perfect dataset. But you can often identify observable proxies for the concepts you care about. For example, if you want to measure “state capacity,” you might look at tax revenue as a percentage of GDP, the number of government employees per capita, or the frequency of public service delivery failures. Each proxy has limitations, but together they can provide a more complete picture of government capacity, without actually having a formal measure.

Start with the Research Question

Before searching for data, break the research question into its major components.

Consider the question:

How have Chinese infrastructure investments affected public attitudes toward China in African countries since 2010?

This question contains several searchable components:

Component Examples of Search Concepts
Actor China, Chinese government, Chinese firms, Belt and Road Initiative
Activity Infrastructure investment, development finance, foreign direct investment, construction projects
Outcome Public opinion, attitudes, favorability, trust, sentiment
Location Africa, African countries, specific country names
Time period 2010–present
Unit of analysis Individual survey respondent, country-year, infrastructure project, province, district
Possible comparison Areas with and without Chinese projects; before and after investment

Each component suggests different data needs. The investment activity may require project-level financial data, while the outcome may require public-opinion surveys. Geographic analysis may require project coordinates and subnational administrative boundaries.

TipConvert the Question into a Data Requirements Table

Before opening a search engine, create a table like the following:

Information Needed Ideal Measure Possible Data Source Likely Limitation
Chinese infrastructure investment Project value, location, start date, sector AidData, government records, company reports Missing projects or uncertain values
Public attitudes toward China Survey response measuring favorability Afrobarometer or national surveys Inconsistent questions across years
Local socioeconomic conditions Income, population, urbanization World Bank, national statistics, satellite data Different geographic units
Political conditions Regime type, election timing, conflict V-Dem, ACLED, election databases Measurement uncertainty

This step helps prevent unfocused searching and makes it easier to recognize when a dataset is useful.

Concepts, Variables, and Search Terms

Researchers search more effectively when they distinguish between a concept and the terms that may be used to describe it.

A concept such as political instability may appear in data sources under terms such as:

  • protests;
  • riots;
  • civil unrest;
  • political violence;
  • demonstrations;
  • coups;
  • government turnover;
  • regime instability;
  • state fragility; or
  • conflict events.

Different organizations may use different terminology for similar phenomena. A narrow search using only one phrase can therefore miss relevant sources.

Build a Search Vocabulary

For each major concept, identify:

  1. Synonyms — words with similar meanings.
  2. Broader terms — categories that include the concept.
  3. Narrower terms — specific forms of the concept.
  4. Related terms — concepts that are not identical but commonly appear alongside it.
  5. Institutional terminology — language used by governments, international organizations, or industries.
  6. Acronyms and abbreviations — both formal and informal.
  7. Alternative spellings — including British and American spelling.
  8. Historical terminology — names or categories used in earlier periods.
  9. Local-language terms — especially when researching non-English-speaking countries.

For example:

Concept Possible Search Terms
Artificial intelligence AI, machine learning, deep learning, automated decision systems, algorithmic systems
Government spending public expenditure, government expenditure, budget outlays, fiscal spending
Cyberattack cyber attack, computer network attack, data breach, intrusion, ransomware incident
Migration immigration, emigration, displacement, refugee flows, population movement
Military power defense capabilities, armed forces, military capacity, force structure, combat power
NoteSearch Terms Are Not Measures

Finding a source that uses the phrase “military power” does not mean the source measures military power in a way that fits your project.

One dataset may measure military power using military expenditure. Another may use troop numbers, equipment inventories, technological capacity, logistics, or an index combining several indicators. Always inspect the source’s operational definition and coding procedures. This will tell you exactly how the concept is measured.

Part VI: How Search Engines Work

Crawling, Indexing, and Ranking

Search engines operate through three core processes:

  1. Crawling — automated bots traverse the web by following hyperlinks and discovering pages.
  2. Indexing — discovered content is analyzed, parsed, categorized, and stored in the search engine’s database.
  3. Ranking — when a query is submitted, the engine scores indexed pages according to relevance, authority, usability, freshness, location, and other signals.

These processes have important implications for researchers, as being able to manipulate the search engine’s behavior can improve the quality of results (saving time and improving your products!)

A search engine does not search the entire internet in real time. It searches its own index of content that its crawlers have discovered and chosen to store. Some materials may be:

  • too new to have been indexed;
  • blocked from crawlers;
  • hidden behind a login;
  • stored in a database that generates pages only after a query;
  • poorly linked from other websites;
  • available only through an application programming interface;
  • buried below sponsored advertisements or other content;
  • contained in scanned files;
  • located in an archive;
  • removed from the live web; or
  • intentionally excluded from search results.

This means that “I could not find it on Google” is not equivalent to “it does not exist.” If you can’t find something, it means you need to change your search strategy.

The Surface Web and the Deep Web

The surface web consists of pages that ordinary search engines can discover and index.

The deep web includes online content that is not readily indexed, such as:

  • subscription databases;
  • password-protected portals;
  • dynamically generated database results;
  • internal government systems;
  • academic library collections;
  • cloud storage;
  • private company databases;
  • API-accessible data;
  • archived records; and
  • pages blocked through technical settings.

The deep web is not inherently illegal or suspicious. Much of the world’s most valuable research data is part of the deep web because it is stored in structured databases rather than ordinary webpages. It also accounts for an estimated 90% to 99% of the internet’s content.

The dark web is a much smaller category involving networks designed to obscure the location or identity of users and services. It is not a synonym for the deep web.

WarningSearch Results Are Curated, Not Neutral

Search rankings are influenced by algorithms, commercial incentives, location, language, search history, popularity, search-engine optimization, and content availability.

The first result is not necessarily:

  • the most accurate source;
  • the original source;
  • the most complete dataset;
  • the most recent version;
  • the most methodologically appropriate source; or
  • the least biased source.

Search engines help locate evidence. They do not evaluate the evidence for you.

Part VII: Developing a Search Strategy

Search Broadly, Then Narrow

A productive search often moves through several stages, from broad exploratory searching to targeted searching, source verification, gap searching, and citation chaining. This process is iterative: new sources may suggest new search terms, which may lead to new sources, and so on. Additionally, your search strategy will evolve as you learn more about the topic, the available data, and the limitations of those data.

Stage 1: Exploratory Searching

Begin with broad queries to learn how organizations and scholars describe the topic. You can also start with reading broader literature to identify key terms, organizations, and datasets. This stage is about discovery, not precision.

Example Search Parameters:

Chinese infrastructure investment Africa data
global cyber incident database
public opinion China Africa survey
military expenditure dataset country year

At this stage, the goal is not necessarily to find the final dataset. The goal is to discover:

  • common terminology;
  • major organizations;
  • dataset names;
  • prominent researchers;
  • relevant reports;
  • data portals;
  • variables used in prior studies; and
  • references to original sources.

Stage 2: Targeted Searching

Once important terms and organizations are known, narrow the search.

AidData Chinese development finance dataset methodology
site:afrobarometer.org China survey data
site:worldbank.org military expenditure csv
"cyber incident database" methodology

Stage 3: Source Verification

After locating a possible dataset, search specifically for its documentation.

"dataset name" codebook
"dataset name" methodology
"dataset name" data dictionary
"dataset name" validation
"dataset name" limitations
"dataset name" replication
"dataset name" version history

Stage 4: Gap Searching

Identify what is missing and search for complementary sources.

For example:

Chinese infrastructure project coordinates Africa
Africa subnational population raster data
African administrative boundaries shapefile
Afrobarometer geocoded survey data access

Stage 5: Citation Chaining

Use a strong article, report, or dataset as a starting point.

  • Backward citation searching examines the sources cited by the item.
  • Forward citation searching identifies later publications that cited the item.
  • Author searching finds additional work by the same researchers.
  • Dataset citation searching identifies studies that used the same data.
  • Institution searching identifies related products from the organization.

Citation chaining is often more productive than repeatedly changing keywords.

TipKeep a Search Log

A search log will allow for you to document the process so that you can go back and identify gaps in your searches, to redo searches if there is something that you missed, and to better document your research. The below is just an example, please tailor it to your own needs.

Date Search Tool Query Useful Result Decision
2026-08-20 Google "Chinese development finance" Africa dataset AidData portal Review methodology
2026-08-20 Google Scholar China infrastructure African public opinion Several journal articles Examine cited datasets
2026-08-21 JMU Library Chinese investment Africa survey Afrobarometer studies Locate survey files

A search log demonstrates that the search was systematic and helps you avoid repeating unsuccessful queries.

Part VIII: Advanced Search Operators

Search operators allow researchers to control which pages appear in the results. Operator support varies by search engine, and some operators may not work consistently across all platforms.

Exact Phrase Searching

Use quotation marks to search for an exact sequence of words. That said, you will want to avoid quotation marks when you want the search engine to include synonyms or alternate word orders.

Search Parameters

"artificial intelligence readiness index"
"Chinese development finance"
"global terrorism database"

Exact phrase searching is useful for:

  • dataset titles
  • report titles
  • distinctive quotations
  • policy names
  • organization names
  • etc.

Excluding Terms

Use a minus sign to remove unwanted results. Do not place a space between the minus sign and the excluded term, or it won’t work.

Search Parameters

python network analysis -snake
jaguar population data -car
Georgia election data -country

Searching Within a Website or Domain

Use site: to restrict results to a website or domain.

Search Parameters

site:worldbank.org governance indicators
site:un.org refugee data
site:gov "artificial intelligence"
site:mil China strategy filetype:pdf
site:edu conflict dataset

You can use site: with:

  • a complete domain: site:worldbank.org;
  • a subdomain: site:data.worldbank.org;
  • a broad domain type: site:gov;
  • a country-code domain: site:gov.uk; or
  • a specific organization.
NoteBe Careful with Domain Assumptions

A .org domain is not automatically nonpartisan, a .gov source is not automatically complete, and a .edu page is not necessarily peer reviewed.

Domain type provides context, not proof of quality.

Searching for File Types

Use filetype: to find specific document formats.

Search Parameters

filetype:csv military expenditure
filetype:xlsx election results
filetype:pdf "annual threat assessment"
filetype:json conflict events
filetype:geojson administrative boundaries
filetype:shp population boundaries
filetype:zip satellite data

Common file types include:

Operator Typical Content
filetype:csv Tabular data
filetype:xlsx Excel workbooks
filetype:json Structured web or API data
filetype:xml Structured records
filetype:pdf Reports, documentation, codebooks
filetype:pptx Briefings and presentations
filetype:docx Reports and administrative documents
filetype:zip Compressed datasets
filetype:geojson Geographic vector data
filetype:kml Geographic features
filetype:shp References to shapefile components

A shapefile normally consists of several files packaged together, so searching for filetype:zip may be more effective than searching only for .shp.

Searching Page Titles

Use intitle: when an important term should appear in the page title.

Search Parameters

intitle:dataset cyber incidents
intitle:"annual report" OPEC
intitle:codebook migration
intitle:methodology corruption index

Use allintitle: when all listed terms should appear in the title.

allintitle: China Africa investment dataset

Searching URLs

Use inurl: to search for terms within a web address.

Search Parameters

inurl:data conflict events
inurl:download population estimates
inurl:api election results
inurl:dataset China trade

This can help locate sections of large websites that are dedicated to data, downloads, or APIs.

Combining Search Operators

Operators become more powerful when combined.

Search Parameters

site:gov filetype:xlsx "foreign assistance"
site:un.org filetype:csv refugee population
site:edu intitle:dataset "political violence"
site:worldbank.org inurl:download filetype:zip boundaries
"China" "public opinion" Africa filetype:pdf after:2020

Do not over-constrain the first search. If a query returns no useful results, remove one operator or substitute a broader term.

Boolean Logic

Many academic databases support Boolean operators.

  • AND requires both terms.
  • OR accepts either term.
  • NOT excludes a term.
  • Parentheses group related terms.

Example:

Search Parameters

(China OR Chinese OR "Belt and Road") AND
(investment OR infrastructure OR "development finance") AND
(Africa OR Kenya OR Nigeria OR Ethiopia)

Search engines do not all interpret Boolean logic in exactly the same way. Google and Google Scholar both support Boolean operators, but to make them function correctly on these sites they must be typed in ALL CAPS. Library databases usually provide more consistent Boolean support than general web search engines.

Wildcards and Truncation

Some academic databases allow a wildcard or truncation symbol.

Search Parameters

migrat*

This may return:

  • migration;
  • migrant;
  • migrants;
  • migratory; and
  • migrating.

The supported symbol varies. Common symbols include *, ?, and $. Consult the database’s help page before relying on truncation.

Proximity Searching

Some databases allow researchers to find words appearing near one another at intervals you are able to specify.

Search Parameters

China NEAR/5 infrastructure
cyber W/3 attack
artificial intelligence N5 military

The syntax varies by platform and may not work on all platforms. Proximity searches are useful when two terms are related but may not appear as an exact phrase.

Date Restrictions

Some search engines support date operators or provide date filters through the interface.

Search Parameters

"climate change" after:2023
"annual threat assessment" before:2025
"artificial intelligence strategy" after:2024-01-01

Date restrictions should be used cautiously. A webpage’s displayed or indexed date may represent:

  • original publication;
  • most recent update;
  • migration to a new website;
  • metadata creation; or
  • the date the search engine discovered the page.

Always verify the date within the source itself.

Part X: Finding Data Through Documents

Datasets are often referenced indirectly in reports and articles.

A report may contain a sentence such as:

Data were collected from the Ministry of Finance’s annual procurement database.

That sentence provides a lead even when the report does not include the data itself.

When reviewing a document, search within it for:

data
dataset
methodology
source
appendix
download
repository
codebook
supplement
replication
API
CSV
Excel

Pay particular attention to:

  • footnotes;
  • appendices;
  • methods sections;
  • data availability statements;
  • acknowledgments;
  • references;
  • supplementary materials; and
  • captions beneath tables and figures.
TipFollow the Source Trail

Do not stop at a chart reproduced in a report.

Trace the evidence backward:

  1. Who created the chart?
  2. What dataset was used?
  3. Who originally collected the observations?
  4. Is the original dataset available?
  5. Has the data been transformed?
  6. Are the methods documented?
  7. Is a newer version available?

The report may be a secondary source even when it appears authoritative.

Part XIV: Using AI-Assisted Search

AI tools can help researchers:

  • brainstorm search terms;
  • identify possible data repositories;
  • translate search queries;
  • summarize documentation;
  • generate API request examples;
  • explain unfamiliar file formats;
  • develop code for cleaning data; and
  • compare possible measurement strategies.

However, an AI system may:

  • invent a dataset;
  • provide an obsolete URL;
  • confuse similarly named sources;
  • misstate geographic or temporal coverage;
  • omit licensing restrictions;
  • inaccurately describe variables; or
  • cite a source that does not support the claim. :::

Treat AI Output as a Lead, Not Evidence

When an AI system recommends a dataset:

  1. Visit the official source.
  2. Confirm that the dataset exists.
  3. Inspect the documentation.
  4. Verify the variables and coverage.
  5. Confirm the current version.
  6. Review the license.
  7. Record the actual source, not merely the AI response.

A useful AI prompt might be:

I am studying whether Chinese infrastructure projects influence public
attitudes toward China in African countries. Suggest categories of data
I would need, possible types of repositories to search, alternative search
terms, and likely measurement limitations. Do not invent specific datasets.

This uses AI to improve the search process without treating it as an authoritative source.

Part XV: Evaluating a Potential Dataset

Locating a dataset is only the beginning. The researcher must determine whether it is suitable for the project.

Relevance

Ask:

  • Does the dataset measure the concept in the research question?
  • Are the variables direct measures or proxies?
  • Does the unit of analysis match the study?
  • Does the dataset cover the relevant population?
  • Are the geographic areas appropriate?
  • Does the time period fit the question?

A dataset can be high quality but irrelevant to a particular research design.

Provenance

Data provenance describes where the data originated and how they reached their current form.

Identify:

  • who originally collected the data;
  • who processed the data;
  • who currently distributes the data;
  • whether multiple sources were combined;
  • what transformations occurred;
  • whether the raw observations are available; and
  • whether the source chain is documented.

A dataset downloaded from a university website may still originate from government records, news reports, sensors, surveys, or another database.

Authority and Expertise

Consider:

  • Who created the dataset?
  • Does the organization have relevant expertise?
  • Are the authors identifiable?
  • Is the project affiliated with a credible institution?
  • Is the methodology publicly available?
  • Has the dataset been used in peer-reviewed research?
  • Is there a process for reporting errors?

Authority should be evaluated alongside transparency. A prestigious organization can still produce poorly documented data.

Coverage

Examine:

  • geographic coverage;
  • temporal coverage;
  • population coverage;
  • sector coverage;
  • event coverage;
  • language coverage;
  • frequency of observation;
  • known exclusions; and
  • changes in coverage over time.

Do not assume that a dataset described as “global” includes every country or territory.

Completeness

Questions include:

  • Are records missing?
  • Are some countries or years more complete than others?
  • Are values absent because nothing occurred or because nothing was reported?
  • Are missing-value codes documented?
  • Are recent periods incomplete because of reporting delays?
  • Are some fields optional?

Distinguish carefully among:

  • zero;
  • missing;
  • unknown;
  • not applicable;
  • not reported; and
  • not yet available.

These values do not mean the same thing.

Accuracy

Accuracy concerns how closely the recorded value reflects the real-world phenomenon.

Potential errors include:

  • data-entry mistakes;
  • inaccurate coordinates;
  • duplicated events;
  • incorrect dates;
  • mistranslation;
  • inconsistent coding;
  • false reports;
  • outdated values;
  • rounding;
  • estimation error; and
  • conversion errors.

Some sources include confidence scores or uncertainty ranges. Preserve these fields when possible.

Reliability

Reliability concerns whether the collection and coding procedures produce consistent results.

Ask:

  • Are coders trained?
  • Is intercoder reliability reported?
  • Are automated classifications validated?
  • Are coding rules clear?
  • Would another researcher classify the observation similarly?
  • Did definitions change over time?

Validity

Validity concerns whether the variable actually represents the intended concept.

For example:

  • Military spending is not identical to military capability.
  • Internet searches are not identical to public opinion.
  • Reported protests are not identical to all protests.
  • Nighttime lights are not identical to economic productivity.
  • Social-media posts are not identical to population sentiment.
  • Patent counts are not identical to technological quality.

A useful proxy can still be valid for a limited purpose, but the researcher must explain the connection between the proxy and the concept.

Timeliness

Determine:

  • when the dataset was last updated;
  • how frequently it is updated;
  • whether recent records are provisional;
  • whether historical records are revised;
  • whether publication lags vary; and
  • whether the project is still maintained.

A dataset can have a recent webpage while containing old observations.

Consistency

Look for changes in:

  • variable definitions;
  • country codes;
  • geographic boundaries;
  • units;
  • currencies;
  • collection methods;
  • survey wording;
  • file structure;
  • classification categories; and
  • source availability.

A time series may appear continuous even though its underlying methodology changed.

Granularity

Granularity refers to the level of detail.

Examples include:

  • daily versus annual;
  • individual versus household;
  • city versus country;
  • transaction versus company;
  • event versus conflict;
  • pixel versus province.

More granular data are not automatically better. Highly detailed data may contain more noise, privacy risk, and missingness.

Documentation

A usable dataset should ideally include:

  • a codebook;
  • variable definitions;
  • collection procedures;
  • source descriptions;
  • inclusion and exclusion rules;
  • missing-value conventions;
  • known limitations;
  • version information;
  • licensing conditions;
  • contact information; and
  • citation instructions.

Poor documentation increases the risk of analytical error.

Accessibility and Format

Consider whether the data are:

  • downloadable;
  • machine readable;
  • searchable;
  • available without a login;
  • restricted to a subscription;
  • provided through an API;
  • limited to an interactive dashboard;
  • available only as a PDF;
  • stored in a proprietary format; or
  • accessible only after an application.

A dashboard may display data without providing the underlying records.

Licensing and Usage Restrictions

Review whether you may:

  • download the data;
  • reproduce the data;
  • modify the data;
  • merge it with other sources;
  • share it with classmates;
  • publish portions of it;
  • post it to GitHub;
  • use it commercially;
  • create derivative products; or
  • redistribute cleaned versions.

Record the license at the time of download because terms may change.

Part XVI: Reading a Codebook

A codebook explains how a dataset is organized and how its variables should be interpreted.

A codebook may include:

  • variable name;
  • variable label;
  • description;
  • data type;
  • unit;
  • allowable values;
  • missing-value codes;
  • source;
  • coding instructions;
  • examples; and
  • notes on comparability.

Example:

Variable Label Values Notes
country_iso3 ISO three-letter country code KEN, NGA, GHA Standardized country identifier
event_date Date of event YYYY-MM-DD May reflect best-known date
event_type Category of event Protest, Riot, Battle Definitions in methodology
fatalities Estimated deaths Nonnegative integer May be revised
source_count Number of sources Integer Not necessarily independent sources
WarningNever Guess What a Code Means

A value of 9, 99, -1, or 999 may represent missing or unknown data rather than a real observation.

Always check the codebook before calculating averages, totals, or statistical models.

Questions to Ask While Reading a Codebook

  1. What does each row represent?
  2. What does each column represent?
  3. Which variable uniquely identifies a row?
  4. Are identifiers stable over time?
  5. Which variables are original and which are derived?
  6. How are missing values encoded?
  7. Are categories mutually exclusive?
  8. Are text labels and numeric codes both provided?
  9. What units are used?
  10. Are values nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio?
  11. Have any variables been discontinued?
  12. Are some variables available only for certain years or cases?

Part XX: Primary, Secondary, and Derived Data

Primary Data

Primary data are collected directly for the research project.

Examples include:

  • surveys;
  • interviews;
  • experiments;
  • field observations;
  • sensor readings;
  • original document coding;
  • image interpretation; and
  • laboratory measurements.

Advantages include control over the measurement process and alignment with the research question. Disadvantages include cost, time, access constraints, ethical requirements, and limited scale.

Secondary Data

Secondary data were collected by someone else.

Examples include:

  • government statistics;
  • existing surveys;
  • administrative records;
  • published event databases;
  • commercial datasets; and
  • archival collections.

Advantages include speed, scale, and lower cost. Disadvantages include limited control over definitions, coverage, and collection procedures.

Derived Data

Derived data are created by transforming other data.

Examples include:

  • indexes;
  • ratios;
  • moving averages;
  • model predictions;
  • geocoded records;
  • sentiment scores;
  • network-centrality measures;
  • interpolated values;
  • satellite-derived estimates; and
  • machine-learning classifications.

Derived variables can be highly useful but introduce additional assumptions. Researchers should document the transformation process and preserve the underlying source variables.

Part XXI: Structured, Semi-Structured, and Unstructured Data

Structured Data

Structured data follow a predefined tabular or relational format.

Examples include:

  • spreadsheets;
  • CSV files;
  • relational databases;
  • survey matrices; and
  • statistical files.

Structured data are usually easiest to analyze.

Semi-Structured Data

Semi-structured data contain organizational markers but do not necessarily follow a simple table.

Examples include:

  • JSON;
  • XML;
  • HTML;
  • log files;
  • email headers; and
  • API responses.

These formats often require parsing before analysis.

Unstructured Data

Unstructured data do not arrive as rows and columns.

Examples include:

  • reports;
  • speeches;
  • articles;
  • photographs;
  • satellite images;
  • audio;
  • video;
  • social-media posts; and
  • interview transcripts.

Unstructured data can be converted into structured variables through:

  • manual coding;
  • natural-language processing;
  • computer vision;
  • transcription;
  • named-entity recognition;
  • topic modeling;
  • sentiment analysis; and
  • supervised classification.

The conversion process itself becomes part of the research methodology.

Part XXIV: Common Search Failures

Searching Only the Exact Research Question

A full research question may be too complex for a search engine.

Instead of:

Does Chinese infrastructure investment improve African public opinion
toward China after controlling for economic development?

search the components separately:

Chinese infrastructure projects Africa dataset
Afrobarometer attitudes toward China
Africa district economic indicators
geocoded Chinese development finance

Using Only One Vocabulary

A search for foreign aid may miss datasets categorized as:

  • development finance;
  • official development assistance;
  • grants;
  • concessional lending;
  • project finance;
  • overseas development;
  • development cooperation; or
  • foreign assistance.

Searching Only Google

General search engines may not index:

  • library databases;
  • subscription resources;
  • dynamic portals;
  • archival collections;
  • APIs;
  • replication repositories; or
  • local-language sources.

Downloading Without Reading the Documentation

A file may look usable while containing:

  • coded missing values;
  • estimated records;
  • duplicated observations;
  • partial coverage;
  • incompatible units;
  • changed definitions; or
  • restricted variables.

Assuming the File Name Describes the Contents

A file named global_data.csv may contain only 50 countries or five years.

Inspect the actual rows, columns, metadata, and documentation.

Treating a Dashboard as the Dataset

Interactive dashboards may:

  • apply hidden filters;
  • round values;
  • display only recent years;
  • omit uncertainty;
  • aggregate observations;
  • change over time; or
  • prevent bulk download.

Look for a download page, API, or source-data link.

Ignoring Version Information

Datasets are revised. A later release may:

  • add cases;
  • correct errors;
  • change variable names;
  • recode historical observations;
  • remove records;
  • alter geographic boundaries; or
  • adopt a new methodology.

Record the version or download date.

Failing to Save the Original Data

Websites disappear and files are replaced. Preserve the original file whenever licensing permits.

Do not overwrite it with the cleaned version.

Confusing “No Results” with “No Data”

An unsuccessful search may mean:

  • the terminology is wrong;
  • the data use another language;
  • the data are stored in a report;
  • the source is not indexed;
  • access requires a library;
  • the data are available through an API;
  • the organization must be contacted;
  • the records are restricted; or
  • the data genuinely do not exist.

Why Record Rejected Sources?

Rejected sources help demonstrate that the researcher:

  • considered alternatives;
  • made deliberate methodological choices;
  • recognized important limitations;
  • did not select data solely because they supported an expected conclusion; and
  • can revisit earlier options if the design changes.

Part XXVIII: Contacting Data Owners

Sometimes the data exist but are not publicly downloadable. If you run into this, you can consider contacting the organization that maintains the data to see if they will share it with you. While not all will be willing to share (as the authors may be using the data for their own research), some may be willing to provide access if you explain your research question and how you intend to use the data.

A professional request should explain:

  • who you are;
  • your institutional affiliation;
  • the research topic;
  • the specific data requested;
  • the intended use;
  • the desired format;
  • whether the results will be published;
  • how the source will be credited; and
  • any relevant deadline.

Avoid asking vaguely for “all of your data.” Demonstrate that you have reviewed the public materials and know what you need.

A data owner may:

  • provide the data;
  • direct you to an existing source;
  • offer a limited version;
  • require an agreement;
  • charge a fee;
  • request a research proposal;
  • prohibit redistribution; or
  • decline the request.

Record any restrictions attached to the data.

Part XXX: Ethical Data Discovery

Researchers must consider not only whether data can be found, but whether they should be collected, combined, or published.

Questions include:

  • Does the dataset contain personally identifiable information?
  • Could individuals be reidentified?
  • Does combining datasets create new privacy risks?
  • Does the source involve vulnerable populations?
  • Was meaningful consent obtained?
  • Could publication expose people to harm?
  • Are locations sensitive?
  • Does the dataset reflect surveillance or coercive collection?
  • Are protected or classified materials involved?
  • Does the project require institutional review?
WarningMosaic Effects

Separate pieces of non-sensitive information can become sensitive when combined.

For example, occupation, approximate location, event date, and organizational affiliation may collectively identify an individual even when names have been removed.

Data Minimization

Collect only the data needed to answer the research question.

Do not retain sensitive fields merely because they are available.

Consider:

  • removing direct identifiers;
  • reducing geographic precision;
  • aggregating small categories;
  • limiting access;
  • encrypting sensitive files;
  • separating identifiers from analytical data;
  • defining a retention period; and
  • documenting who may use the data.

Part XXXIV: Key Takeaways

Finding data is an analytical process rather than a clerical task.

The most important principles are:

  1. Begin with the research question, not the first available dataset.
  2. Translate concepts into multiple search terms and possible measures.
  3. Search across general engines, specialized databases, repositories, archives, APIs, and institutional websites.
  4. Trace secondary sources back to the original data.
  5. Read the codebook and methodology before conducting analysis.
  6. Evaluate relevance, validity, reliability, coverage, completeness, and timeliness.
  7. Record sources that were considered but rejected.
  8. Preserve raw data and document every transformation.
  9. Review licensing, privacy, ethical, and legal constraints.
  10. Treat unavailable or incomplete data as a substantive limitation of the research design.

Suggested Readings

All readings are journal or news articles.

Adcock, R., & Collier, D. (2001). Measurement validity: A shared standard for qualitative and quantitative research. American Political Science Review, 95(3), 529–546. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055401003100

The definitive treatment of measurement validity in social science research. Directly applicable to operationalization and variable selection for intelligence research designs.

Tetlock, P. E. (1999). Theory-driven reasoning about plausible pasts and probable futures in world politics. American Journal of Political Science, 43(2), 335–366. https://doi.org/10.2307/2991799

Examines how analysts’ theoretical frameworks shape what hypotheses they consider and what evidence they weight.

Cederman, L. E. (2003). Modeling the size of wars: From billiard balls to sandpiles. American Political Science Review, 97(1), 135–150. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055403000571

Applies complex systems and self-organized criticality to war size distributions. Formal demonstration that conflict systems exhibit tipping point dynamics inconsistent with standard linear models.
