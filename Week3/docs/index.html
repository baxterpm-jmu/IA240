Week 3: Introduction to Data Science and the Analytical Workflow

IA 240: Technology Applications in a Networked World

Data science is the methodological foundation for systematic, rigorous analysis. Before learning specific statistical techniques, analysts need to understand the workflow those techniques serve: how analytical problems are framed, how the data science process is structured, and how different data types shape what analysis is possible. This lecture introduces the data science process and the analytical workflow, with a focus on how to frame problems, identify data types, and understand the implications of those data types for your analytical approach.

Part I: What is Data Science?

Defining the Field

Data science is the multidisciplinary field concerned with extracting knowledge and insight from data—structured or unstructured, large or small. It draws on mathematics, statistics, computer science, and domain expertise to turn raw information into actionable understanding.

You engage with this everyday, actually. Can you think of how data science is present in your daily life?

NoteThe Core Insight

Data science is what enables us to move from large, incomprehensible datasets to actionable knowledge. The goal is not the technique, as the techique may vary from problem set to problem; rather, it is the insight the technique produces.

Core Functions

A practicing data scientist (or a data-literate analyst) draws on competencies across several domains:

Function Description Relevance to IA
Mathematics & Applied Mathematics Foundations of algorithms, optimization, and statistical theory Understanding what analytical tools actually compute
Computer Programming (R, Python, Julia, SQL) Implementing analysis, automating tasks, querying databases Scale: computers process millions of records in seconds
Data Mining Discovering patterns in large datasets without predefined hypotheses Identifying anomalies, clusters, or trends in raw intelligence data
Database Management Storing, organizing, and querying structured data Working with IC data warehouses and relational databases
Machine Learning Building systems that improve from data Automated classification, prediction, and anomaly detection
Data Visualization Communicating findings through charts, maps, and dashboards Presenting analysis to policymakers and consumers
Applied Statistics Formal inference, hypothesis testing, uncertainty quantification Calibrating confidence in analytic judgments

Obviously, every problem set is going to require different combinations of these skills. A project that has data already collected and cleaned may require only statistical modeling and visualization, while a project that needs unstructured data is going to require much more mining and programming expertise.

Data Science in Intelligence Analysis

WarningCourse Framing — Not Training Data Scientists

The goal of this course is not to make you a data scientist. The goal is to make you a data-literate analyst who understands how to connect data of all kinds to analytical problems. That means knowing when to use a tool, what it assumes, and how to interpret its output, even if you are not the one writing the code.

Applications of data science methods in intelligence analysis include:

  • Data Integration and Fusion — combining HUMINT, SIGINT, OSINT, and GEOINT into unified pictures
  • Text Analysis and NLP — extracting meaning from foreign-language documents, social media, and intercepted communications
  • Social Network Analysis — mapping relationships between individuals, organizations, and entities
  • Geospatial Analysis — identifying spatial patterns in conflict, migration, or economic activity
  • Machine Learning for Pattern Recognition — flagging anomalies in financial flows, communications metadata, or imagery
  • Predictive Analytics — forecasting conflict onset, proliferation timelines, or election outcomes
  • Cybersecurity — detecting intrusions, attributing attacks, and modeling adversary behavior
  • Simulation and Scenario Analysis — modeling complex systems to explore potential outcomes and inform decision-making
  • Visualization — producing products that communicate complex findings clearly to consumers
  • Risk Assessment — quantifying uncertainty and supporting structured decision-making
  • Early Warning Systems — automated monitoring of indicators that precede significant events
NoteMindset vs. Technique

When we approach problems/questions, we want to think about the data that we would need to fully understand and assess the situtation. This is a mindset that is applicable to all types of data, not just quantitative data. The techniques we will learn in this course are tools to help us analyze the data we have, but they are not the end goal for this course.

Part II: The Data Science Process

A Standard Workflow

Data science projects follow a recognizable lifecycle. Understanding this cycle is as important as any individual technique, because most analytical failures occur at the transitions between stages—not within them.

The following stages describe the approach used in this program:

  1. Problem Statement
    Before collecting a single data point, define what you are trying to answer. A problem statement is an explanation of the gap between what is known and what needs to be known, and why that gap matters. In intelligence, this is often framed as: What is the probability that X will occur? or What factors are driving Y? A weak problem statement produces a weak analysis regardless of data quality.
  2. Identification & Procurement
    Identify what data exists that is relevant to the problem. Determine how to obtain it—open sources, databases, collection operations, surveys. Document provenance, collection methodology, and known limitations before analysis begins. Critical thinking and creativity are key elements here!
  3. Selection
    Not all available data is relevant. Select the variables, time ranges, geographic units, and sources that directly help you understand and address the problem statement. This decision embeds analytical judgment (i.e., what you exclude matters as much as what you include).
  4. Preprocessing & Transformation
    Clean the data (address missing values, inconsistencies, and errors), standardize formats, and transform variables for analysis (e.g., converting categorical to numerical, creating ratio variables, normalizing scales). Unfortunately, this is typically 80% of total project time! 😢
  5. EDA and Modeling
    Explore the data visually and statistically to understand its structure and relationships before applying formal models. Then apply appropriate analytical or statistical methods to test hypotheses or discover patterns.
  6. Interpretation
    Translate statistical outputs into intelligence judgments. What do the results mean for the problem statement? What is the confidence level? What alternative explanations have been considered?
  7. Knowledge / Dissemination
    Communicate findings in a form appropriate to the consumer—written product, briefing, dashboard, or visualization.

  • How does this workflow fit into the intelligence cycle we discussed previously?
  • Is this workflow linear or iterative? Why does that matter for intelligence analysis?

Part III: Problem Statements

What Makes a Good Problem Statement?

A problem statement is the analytical foundation of every data-driven project. It must be:

  • Specific — describes a precise gap in knowledge, not a broad topic
  • Significant — explains why the gap matters to a decision-maker or stakeholder (or other relevant audience)
  • Researchable — can be addressed with available (or acquirable) data
  • Bounded — scoped so that it can be answered within available time and resources

Weak Problem Statement

“I want to study terrorism.”

This is a topic, not a problem statement. It does not specify what question is being asked, what data could answer it, or why the answer matters.

Stronger Problem Statement

“It is unclear whether economic grievances or ideological affiliation is the stronger predictor of individual radicalization in Western Europe between 2010 and 2023. Identifying the dominant pathway has direct implications for counter-terrorism resource allocation.”

This specifies the gap, the scope, the data needed, and the policy relevance.

NoteThe “Identifying the Unexpected” Approach

Problem statements sometimes emerge not from pre-specified questions but from anomalies – the “hey, that’s weird” signal – or patterns that deviate from baseline/expected. A rigorous EDA phase often surfaces these unexpected signals, which then become the basis of a new problem statement and deeper analytical effort.

This does not always have to originate in the data, though. Think about moments where you noticed something unexpected in your own life or when you were reading the news or a reading for a class. These can lead to the best research questions of all - the why questions.

ImportantProblem Statement vs. Research Questions vs. Intelligence Question

In the IA program, you were hear a variety of terms used to describe the analytical problem you are trying to assess, including research question, problem statement, and intelligence question. While not interchangeable, these terms are related and often used together in the analytical workflow.

Problem Statement It explains what is not sufficiently understood, why that gap matters, and why further investigation is needed.

Despite growing investment in AI-enabled intelligence tools, limited evidence exists about how analysts decide whether to trust algorithmic outputs. This gap may hinder the effective and responsible integration of AI into intelligence analysis.

Research Question Translates the broader problem into a specific, answerable question that guides systematic research. It is designed to produce generalizable knowledge or improve theoretical understanding.

What factors influence intelligence analysts’ willingness to trust AI-generated assessments?

Intelligence Question Asks for decision-relevant information about a specific actor, event, risk, or development. It is usually narrower, time-sensitive, and actionable.

How is China using AI-enabled tools to improve intelligence collection and analysis, and how will this impact their intelligence capabilities in the next 3-5 years?

Part IV: Understanding Data Types in an Intelligence Context

Some Categories

Qualitative

  • Interview transcripts
  • Intelligence reports
  • Social media posts
  • Diplomatic cables
  • Images and videos (unprocessed)

Strengths: depth, context, nuance, access to meaning and intention

Limitations: time-consuming to collect and analyze; harder to standardize; analyst interpretation bias; selective coding

Quantitative

  • Population sizes
  • Crime statistics
  • Economic indicators
  • Sensor readings
  • Satellite imagery pixel values (processed)

Strengths: objective, replicable, aggregable, comparable across time and place

Limitations: can oversimplify complex phenomena; vulnerable to missing variables; sampling bias; measurement errors

Unstructured

  • Raw satellite imagery
  • Social media video
  • Intercepted audio
  • Web scraped text

Strengths: captures real-world signals in near-real-time; essential for modern OSINT and GEOINT

Limitations: requires expensive tools and specialized processing; data-source bias; limited accessibility without technical infrastructure

Structured

  • Databases
  • Spreadsheets
  • Structured reports

Strengths: easy to query, filter, and analyze; supports statistical modeling; can be integrated with other structured datasets

Limitations: may omit context; can be rigid and slow to adapt to new information; may not capture unanticipated signals; may be formatted in a way that is not compatible with other datasets

Key Considerations for Analysts

Reliability
Are the data collected and measured consistently? If you use the same instrument at different times or by different collectors, do you get the same result? Inconsistent collection procedures undermine comparability.
Validity
Does the data actually measure what you claim it measures? A dataset of diplomatic cable volume may be reliable (consistently counted) but invalid as a measure of diplomatic engagement if cables are not the primary communication channel.
Triangulation
Combining multiple data types and sources to strengthen findings and reduce blind spots. When qualitative HUMINT, quantitative economic indicators, and IMINT all point in the same direction, confidence increases. When they diverge, that divergence is itself analytically significant.
Ethical Issues
Unstructured and OSINT data can often involve private individuals who did not consent to intelligence collection. Privacy, misrepresentation, and manipulation risks must be weighed, especially when data informs operations or policy that affects those individuals.

Part V: The Data Workflow and Analytical Process

Why a Disciplined Workflow Matters

Analysis deals with noisy, incomplete, and uncertain data collected (sometimes under adversarial conditions). Without a disciplined analytical process:

  • Conclusions may be driven by assumptions rather than evidence
  • Errors introduced in early stages compound through the analysis
  • Results cannot be reviewed, replicated, or challenged by colleagues or consumers
  • Bias may go undetected, leading to flawed judgments and decisions
  • Effort put in may not be utilized effectively, leading to wasting time and resources on irrelevant data or methods

A documented workflow makes analysis traceable, reproducible, and auditable.

Core Workflow Stages

  1. Data Collection
    Gather raw data from classified, commercial, or open sources. Includes structured (databases), unstructured (text, images), and semi-structured (JSON, logs) data. Document every source you use (and those that you don’t, as it may be relevant later on), the bibliographical information, why you think it will be useful, and what is a limitation of the data. I would also recommend downloading the data, as sometimes websites change or go down, and you want to be able to replicate your analysis later on.
    I realize this seems like a lot of documenting, but it will be critically important as you progress through the IA program and in your career.
  2. Data Cleaning and Preprocessing
    Remove errors, fill gaps, and standardize values to make data usable. Common issues: missing values (impute, interpolate, or flag), duplicates (identify by unique ID and remove), inconsistent formats (standardize date/time and coordinate formats), outliers (assess whether they are real signals or data errors), noise (filter irrelevant text, bot activity, or spam).
  3. Data Transformation and Integration
    Convert raw data into analytical formats. Align units, time intervals, and coordinate systems. Aggregate or disaggregate to the appropriate level of analysis (e.g., event-level → country-year). Combine multiple datasets through join operations; validate joins by manually spot-checking records.
  4. Analysis and Modeling
    Apply statistical or computational techniques to discover meaning. Combine descriptive summaries with inferential or predictive models.
  5. Validation and Quality Assurance
    Check results for plausibility. Run sensitivity analyses—do conclusions hold under different assumptions or subsets of data? Seek independent review.
  6. Visualization and Communication
    Translate findings into products appropriate to the consumer. Tailor complexity to audience expertise (Meeting 26).
  7. Feedback and Iteration
    Review the impact and accuracy of analysis. Adjust collection priorities or methods for future cycles. The analytical process is iterative, not linear.

Part VI: AI and the Analytical Workflow

How can the present-day AI models assist in this workflow?

Present-day AI models can support nearly every stage of the analytical workflow, but their most appropriate role is generally to augment human judgment rather than replace it. AI can accelerate repetitive tasks, identify patterns, generate possible interpretations, and help analysts interact with large volumes of information. However, analysts remain responsible for evaluating sources, selecting appropriate methods, validating results, and determining whether a conclusion is sufficiently supported by the available evidence.

At this stage of development, AI is best suited for well-bounded tasks rather than open-ended analysis.

How does AI fit into the Analytical Workflow?

1. Data Collection

AI tools can help analysts locate, retrieve, and organize potentially relevant information. Examples include:

  • Generating search terms, Boolean queries, and alternative spellings or transliterations.
  • Monitoring large numbers of news reports, documents, databases, or social-media posts.
  • Extracting text from documents, images, audio, or video.
  • Identifying named entities such as people, organizations, locations, weapons systems, and events.
  • Translating foreign-language materials to support initial review.
  • Classifying collected material by topic, source, geographic area, or estimated relevance.

AI can greatly expand the amount of material an analyst can review, but it can also increase the amount of irrelevant or low-quality information collected. The analyst must still evaluate the reliability, credibility, access date, provenance, and potential bias of each source. AI-generated summaries should not substitute for examining the original material.

2. Data Cleaning and Preprocessing

AI can assist with repetitive cleaning tasks, particularly when working with text or inconsistently formatted records. It may help:

  • Identify likely duplicates or near-duplicate documents.
  • Standardize dates, place names, organization names, and other values.
  • Extract structured variables from unstructured text.
  • Detect missing, unusual, or potentially erroneous values.
  • Classify spam, bot-generated content, advertisements, or irrelevant material.
  • Generate Python, R, SQL, or spreadsheet formulas for cleaning data.
  • Suggest regular expressions for extracting recurring patterns.

These outputs should be treated as recommendations rather than unquestioned corrections. An AI system may “clean” away an unusual observation that is actually important or may incorrectly decide that two similar names refer to the same person. Preserve the original data and maintain a record of every significant transformation.

3. Data Transformation and Integration

AI models can help convert data into forms that are easier to analyze. They can:

  • Recommend appropriate variables and units of analysis.
  • Reformat text, tables, dates, coordinates, and categorical values.
  • Generate code for reshaping, aggregating, or joining datasets.
  • Suggest possible matching fields across datasets.
  • Assist with entity resolution when names or identifiers differ.
  • Create preliminary coding schemes for qualitative material.
  • Convert narrative reporting into structured event records.

Integration is an especially high-risk stage because incorrect matches can quietly introduce errors into the entire analysis. AI-generated joins and entity matches should therefore include confidence scores or review flags whenever possible. Analysts should manually inspect a sample of matched and unmatched records.

4. Analysis and Modeling

AI can support exploratory, statistical, computational, and qualitative analysis. Depending on the task, it may:

  • Generate descriptive summaries of a dataset.
  • Identify potential trends, clusters, anomalies, or relationships.
  • Recommend statistical tests or modeling approaches.
  • Generate and explain analytical code.
  • Assist with text classification, topic modeling, sentiment analysis, or document comparison.
  • Extract actors and relationships for network analysis.
  • Generate competing hypotheses or alternative explanations.
  • Identify evidence that supports or contradicts an argument.
  • Translate technical model results into accessible language.

Generative AI is particularly useful as a hypothesis-generation tool. For example, an analyst might ask a model to propose several explanations for an observed increase in military activity. These possibilities can help broaden the analyst’s thinking, but they are not findings until they are evaluated against evidence.

AI-generated analysis can sound confident even when it is incorrect. Models may select an inappropriate statistical test, misunderstand the unit of analysis, invent a source, or interpret correlation as causation. Analysts must understand the method well enough to evaluate whether the model’s recommendation is appropriate.

5. Validation and Quality Assurance

AI can serve as a useful—but imperfect—second reviewer. It can be prompted to:

  • Identify weaknesses in an argument.
  • Search for missing assumptions or logical inconsistencies.
  • Generate alternative interpretations of the evidence.
  • Compare findings across models, time periods, or data subsets.
  • Review code for likely errors.
  • Check whether claims are supported by the evidence provided.
  • Create test cases for evaluating an analytical procedure.
  • Simulate how a skeptical reviewer or decision-maker might respond.

One useful approach is to ask the model to challenge the analysis rather than confirm it. For example:

Identify three plausible reasons this conclusion could be wrong. For each reason, explain what additional evidence would increase or decrease confidence in the conclusion.

AI review is NOT independent validation when the system relies on the same data, assumptions, or prompts as the original analysis. Important findings should still be reviewed thoroughly by qualified people and, when possible, tested using independent sources or methods.

6. Visualization and Communication

AI can help analysts transform technical findings into products designed for different audiences. It can:

  • Recommend an appropriate chart or map for a specific analytical question.
  • Generate visualization code.
  • Draft titles, annotations, captions, and explanatory text.
  • Summarize findings at different levels of technical detail.
  • Convert a lengthy analysis into a briefing, executive summary, or talking points.
  • Adapt the same findings for policymakers, technical analysts, or the public.
  • Review whether the main takeaway is clear.
  • Suggest ways to reduce clutter or improve accessibility.

The analyst must ensure that the final product accurately represents the evidence. AI may exaggerate certainty, omit important qualifications, or emphasize the most dramatic finding rather than the most defensible one. Every written claim, number, label, and citation should be verified before dissemination.

7. Feedback and Iteration

AI can help organize feedback and convert it into concrete revisions. For example, it can:

  • Summarize reviewer comments.
  • Group feedback into methodological, substantive, and presentation issues.
  • Identify recurring questions from consumers.
  • Recommend revisions to collection plans.
  • Generate updated code or analytical procedures.
  • Compare different versions of a product.
  • Maintain a structured list of assumptions, limitations, and unresolved questions.

Feedback can also be used to improve how the analyst interacts with the AI system. Prompts, examples, source restrictions, and review procedures should be refined as the analyst learns which tasks the system performs reliably and which require greater supervision.

Appropriate Uses of AI

AI is most valuable when the task involves:

  • Processing a large volume of material.
  • Repeating a clearly defined procedure.
  • Producing a preliminary draft or starting point.
  • Generating multiple alternatives for consideration.
  • Translating between technical and nontechnical language.
  • Helping an analyst explore unfamiliar data or methods.
  • Identifying items that require human review.

AI is less appropriate when the task requires:

  • Access to information the model has not been given.
  • Precise factual recall without source verification.
  • Evaluation of highly ambiguous or deceptive evidence.
  • Understanding sensitive institutional or cultural context.
  • Making legal, ethical, operational, or policy decisions.
  • Assigning final confidence to an intelligence judgment.
  • Processing classified or restricted information in an unauthorized system.

Human Responsibilities

The use of AI does not transfer analytical responsibility to the model. The analyst remains responsible for:

  1. Protecting the data. Do not enter classified, controlled, proprietary, personally identifiable, or otherwise restricted information into an unauthorized AI system.

  2. Verifying the evidence. Trace factual claims to their original sources. Do not cite an AI-generated response as evidence for a substantive claim.

  3. Evaluating the method. Confirm that the selected analytical technique is appropriate for the question, data, and unit of analysis.

  4. Documenting AI use. Record the system used, the task it performed, important prompts or settings, and how its outputs were reviewed or modified.

  5. Preserving reproducibility. Save the original data, code, prompts, outputs, and decision rules needed to reconstruct the analysis.

  6. Communicating uncertainty. Clearly distinguish observed facts, assumptions, model outputs, inferences, and judgments.

  7. Making the final judgment. The analyst—not the AI system—must decide what the evidence supports and how confidently it supports it.

A Practical Human–AI Workflow

A responsible workflow may follow this sequence:

  1. The analyst defines the question, scope, and evidence requirements.
  2. AI assists with a clearly bounded task.
  3. The analyst reviews the output for factual and methodological errors.
  4. Claims are checked against original sources and data.
  5. Code or procedures are tested using known examples.
  6. Important results are replicated or independently reviewed.
  7. The analyst revises the product and documents how AI contributed.
  8. The analyst takes responsibility for the final conclusion.

The central question should therefore not be simply, “Can AI perform this task?” A better set of questions is:

  • What part of the task can AI perform reliably?
  • What information does the system need?
  • How will its output be verified?
  • What errors could it introduce?
  • What decisions must remain with the analyst?

Key Takeaway: AI can make an analytical workflow faster and more scalable, but speed is not the same as rigor. The quality of AI-assisted analysis ultimately depends on the quality of the data, the structure of the task, the analyst’s subject-matter and methodological knowledge, and the strength of the validation process.
